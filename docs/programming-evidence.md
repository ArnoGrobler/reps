# Reps: evidence for workout programming

Research date: 31 October 2026. Scope: how Reps should prescribe sets, reps, rest, work:rest intervals, warm-up, cool-down, weekly volume, frequency, progression and deloads for home training with small equipment (kettlebells, dumbbells, bands, jump rope, sliders, ab wheel, bodyweight).

Every number below traces to a source in section 4 that was opened and checked (PubMed record, PubMed Central full text, or the publisher's or organisation's own document). Anything that could not be checked is in section 5 and is not used to support a number.

**How to read the evidence column**

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Strong | A position stand and at least one systematic review or meta-analysis agree. |
| Moderate | Supported by systematic reviews or controlled trials, but the data are few, mixed, or come mostly from young men. |
| Consensus | An expert body (for example ACSM) or a formal expert panel recommends it, without direct trials testing the exact number. |
| Practitioner | Coaching practice or a value used in study protocols, not itself tested. Use it, but label it as a sensible default. |

**The big picture first.** The newest ACSM position stand (2026, 137 systematic reviews, more than 30,000 people) finds that almost any resistance training works much better than none, and that only a few settings change results: heavier loads, 2 to 3 sets, and 2 or more sessions a week for strength; 10 or more sets per muscle per week for muscle growth [1]. Training to failure, equipment type and periodisation did not consistently change outcomes, and rest length did not change strength [1]. Elastic bands, home-based training and circuit training all improved strength compared with doing nothing [1]. For Reps, the main message is to get the dose and effort right and keep users training; fine-tuning matters much less.

## 1. Summary table: the programming spec

"Region" means one of the four movement groups ACSM suggests covering: upper-body push, upper-body pull, lower-body push (squat, lunge) and lower-body pull (hinge, swing) [1]. "RIR" (reps in reserve) means how many more reps the user could have done before failing; RIR 2 means "stopped with 2 left in the tank" [19].

## 2. Named proven workouts

Each template states whether it was tested in a trial or only comes from expert or practitioner authority. Add the standard Reps warm-up before each one unless the protocol already includes it.

### 2.1 High-intensity circuit, the "7-minute workout" (Klika & Jordan 2013)

- **Equipment:** bodyweight, a wall, a sturdy chair or step.
- **Structure:** 12 exercises, 30 s of work each, 10 s transition, about 7 to 8 min per circuit. Users do as many good reps as they can in each 30 s.

| # | Original exercise (as widely reproduced; see section 5) | Low-impact version used in the 2023 trial (verified) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Jumping jacks | Step jacks |
| 2 | Wall sit | Wall sit |
| 3 | Push-up | Wall push-up |
| 4 | Abdominal crunch | Sit-up with hand reach |
| 5 | Step-up onto chair | Step-up onto an aerobic step |
| 6 | Squat | Half squat |
| 7 | Triceps dip on chair | Bent-knee triceps dip on chair |
| 8 | Plank | Plank (listed as "plank with knee") |
| 9 | High knees, running in place | Hop |
| 10 | Lunge | Split squat |
| 11 | Push-up and rotation | Plank rotation |
| 12 | Side plank | Side plank |

- **Progression (tested):** 3 sessions a week. 1 circuit in week 1, then 1 more circuit every 2 weeks: 2 circuits in weeks 2 to 3, 3 in weeks 4 to 5, 4 in weeks 6 to 7, 5 in week 8. Each session also had a 5 min warm-up and a 5 min cool-down [45].
- **Total time:** 7 to 8 min per circuit; 18 to 50 min per session including warm-up and cool-down, depending on the week.
- **Who it suits:** beginners (low-impact column) to intermediates (original column); conditioning or balanced goals; days with 10 to 45 min.
- **Source and evidence type:** The original is an expert practice article in *ACSM's Health & Fitness Journal*, not a trial [44]. Tested since in controlled trials:
  - 96 recreationally active college students, 3 times a week for 8 weeks; 1 or 2 circuits back to back. Push-up endurance improved; men gained some strength; aerobic fitness improved only in women doing 2 circuits [46].
  - 24 sedentary women with obesity; the modified version above for 8 weeks. Fitness and body composition improved compared with controls [45].
  - Done daily for 6 weeks by adults aged 18 to 30, waist and fat mass went down [47].
- **Limits:** In a lab, the circuit was vigorous but at "the low end" of interval-training intensity, with lower oxygen use and heart rate than cycling intervals [48]. Expect muscular endurance gains more than strength gains.

### 2.2 Whole-body 20/10 intervals ("Tabata-style")

- **Equipment:** bodyweight.
- **Structure:** 8 rounds of 20 s all-out work and 10 s rest, which is 4 min. One exercise per session, rotating between burpees, jumping jacks, mountain climbers and squat thrusts [49]. A second trial used calisthenics at all-out effort in the same 8 x 20/10 format [50].
- **Frequency tested:** 4 times a week for 4 weeks [49]; 3 times a week for 16 weeks [50].
- **Total time:** 4 min plus warm-up.
- **Who it suits:** intermediate and advanced users, conditioning goal, very short days. This format is high-impact and hard on purpose.
- **Source and evidence type:**
  - Controlled trial in 22 recreationally active young women (7 in the interval group). Aerobic fitness (VO2peak) rose about 8%, and push-up and sit-up endurance rose a lot [49].
  - Randomised trial in 55 healthy young men. VO2max improved as much as with treadmill intervals [50].
- **What Tabata actually tested:** The original 1996 study used a mechanically braked exercise bike at about 170% of VO2max: 7 to 8 bouts of 20 s with 10 s rest, until exhaustion, 5 days a week for 6 weeks, in 7 participants. VO2max rose by 7 ml/kg/min and anaerobic capacity by 28% [51]. Bodyweight cannot reproduce that load, so call the Reps version "Tabata-style", not "Tabata".

### 2.3 "5BX" 11-minute bodyweight session (Archila et al. 2021)

- **Equipment:** none.
- **Structure (verified from the full text):**

| Minute | Activity |
|---|---|
| 1 | Warm-up: jumping jacks |
| 2 | Burpees without push-up (hard) |
| 3 | Walk on the spot |
| 4 | High knees, running on the spot (hard) |
| 5 | Walk on the spot |
| 6 | Split squat jumps (hard) |
| 7 | Walk on the spot |
| 8 | High knees, running on the spot (hard) |
| 9 | Walk on the spot |
| 10 | Squat jumps (hard) |
| 11 | Cool-down: walk on the spot |

- **Frequency tested:** 3 times a week for 6 weeks (18 sessions). Each hard minute is done at a self-chosen "challenging" pace; average effort was 82% of maximum heart rate.
- **Who it suits:** beginners to intermediates who want conditioning on a 10 to 15 min day. Offer low-impact swaps (step-backs for burpees, squats for squat jumps) for beginners.
- **Source and evidence type:** Randomised controlled trial in inactive young adults (9 trained, 10 controls). Aerobic fitness improved compared with controls; leg endurance, grip and jump height did not change [54]. The study comes from the Gibala lab, so it is the closest tested bodyweight version of "Gibala-style" low-volume intervals (see 2.8).

### 2.4 Kettlebell swing intervals (Lake & Lauder 2012)

- **Equipment:** one kettlebell. In the study, 12 kg if body mass was under 70 kg, 16 kg if over 70 kg.
- **Structure:** 12 rounds of 30 s kettlebell swings and 30 s rest, which is 12 min.
- **Frequency tested:** 2 times a week for 6 weeks.
- **Who it suits:** intermediate and advanced users who can already swing with good technique; strength-and-power or conditioning goals; anyone with a single moderate kettlebell.
- **Source and evidence type:** Randomised trial in 21 healthy men aged 18 to 27. Half-squat 1RM rose 9.8% and vertical jump rose 19.8% across the two training groups, with no difference between kettlebell swings and jump-squat power training [55].
- **Limits:** Young men only; participants had to be able to half-squat proficiently first. For context, 12 min of continuous two-hand swings with 16 kg pushed college men to about 87% of maximum heart rate but only about 65% of VO2max [57].

### 2.5 Kettlebell 15:15 snatch intervals (Falatic et al. 2015)

- **Equipment:** one kettlebell.
- **Structure:** 20 min of kettlebell snatches, alternating 15 s work and 15 s rest (40 work intervals). Reps per interval were set for each person from a snatch test; the exact rule was not available (section 5).
- **Frequency tested:** 3 times a week for 4 weeks, on top of normal off-season training.
- **Who it suits:** advanced users only. The snatch is a technical lift.
- **Source and evidence type:** Controlled trial in 17 female college (NCAA Division I) soccer players. VO2max rose 2.3 ml/kg/min (about 6%) in the kettlebell group (9 players) and did not change in the circuit weight-training group [56].
- **Limits:** Trained young women, 4 weeks, small sample.

### 2.6 Kettlebell "Simple & Sinister" (Tsatsouline): practitioner authority only

- **Equipment:** one kettlebell (two sizes are useful).
- **Structure (StrongFirst's published standard):**
  - 100 one-arm swings: 10 sets of 10, starting a new set every 30 s and switching hands each set. This takes 5 min.
  - 1 min rest.
  - 10 get-ups: one get-up at the start of each minute, switching sides each minute. This takes 10 min.
  - About 16 min in total [62].
- **Target weights (a test standard, not a starting weight):**

| Level | Women: swing / get-up | Men: swing / get-up |
|---|---|---|
| "Simple" | 24 kg / 16 kg | 32 kg / 32 kg |
| "Sinister" | 32 kg / 24 kg | 48 kg / 48 kg |

  Source: [62].
- **Who it suits:** intermediate and advanced users with a kettlebell who already know the swing and the get-up. In Reps, offer it as a practice structure with whatever bell the user owns. The "every 30 s" and "every minute" timing maps directly onto the timed mode.
- **Evidence type:** No peer-reviewed trial of Simple & Sinister was found. It rests on practitioner authority. The closest research:
  - An uncontrolled pragmatic trial in insufficiently active adults aged 59 to 79 used Tsatsouline's hardstyle method from a different book (*Enter the Kettlebell*). Over 12 weeks (3 supervised 45-min sessions plus 2 home sessions a week) it improved grip strength, 6-minute walk distance, sit-to-stand, and arm-and-leg lean mass, with four non-serious adverse events [61].
  - A scoping review rated the overall kettlebell evidence as low to very low confidence, with no authoritative guidelines [60].
- **Label for the app:** "Popular coaching programme; not tested in trials."

### 2.7 Minimal-dose full-body strength: evidence-derived, not a single tested protocol

- **Equipment:** dumbbells, a kettlebell or bands.
- **Structure:**
  - 2 to 3 sessions a week.
  - One exercise per region: lower push (goblet squat or split squat), lower pull (kettlebell deadlift, hip hinge or swing), upper push (push-up or floor press), upper pull (one-arm row or band row).
  - Pair them as A1/A2 and B1/B2. Do 2 sets of each at RIR 1 to 3 in the rep range for the user's goal, with 60 to 90 s between moves.
  - About 15 to 20 min including a 3 min warm-up.
  - At 2 sessions a week this gives 4 hard sets per region per week, the minimum recommended. Add a third set or a third session to reach 10 or more.
- **Who it suits:** beginners, and anyone on 15 to 20 min days, for strength or balanced goals.
- **Source and evidence type:** Built from review recommendations, not from one trial:
  - At least 4 weekly sets per muscle, priority on multi-joint moves, supersets [27].
  - Minimal-dose approaches improve strength [28] [29].
  - Single sets 2 to 3 times a week raise strength in trained men [30].
  - Supersets save time without losing gains [31].

### 2.8 Not offered as templates, and why

- **Tabata (1996), the original:** needs a mechanically braked bike at about 170% of VO2max [51]. Use 2.2 instead.
- **Gibala-style low-volume intervals:**
  - 8 to 12 rounds of 60 s at about 100% of peak power with 75 s recovery; 6 sessions over 2 weeks in 7 men [52].
  - 3 x 20 s all-out sprints inside a 10 min session; 12 weeks in sedentary men; aerobic fitness rose 19%, the same as 45 min of continuous cycling [53].
  - Both used exercise bikes. The bodyweight version tested by the same group is 2.3 [54].
- **Do they transfer?** Bodyweight and kettlebell interval formats have been tested separately and do improve aerobic fitness [49] [50] [54] [56]. They are harder to dose precisely, and at least one popular bodyweight circuit ran at lower intensity than bike intervals [48].
- **Jay et al. 2011 workplace kettlebell programme** (3 times a week for 8 weeks; less neck, shoulder and low-back pain; stronger trunk extensors; aerobic fitness unchanged [58]) and **Otto et al. 2012** (swings, accelerated swings and goblet squats in sets of 4 to 6 reps, twice a week for 6 weeks; less squat strength gain than weightlifting [59]): the full protocols, loads and rest periods could not be retrieved (section 5).

## 3. Notes per parameter

**Reps for strength.** Network meta-analyses and the 2026 ACSM stand agree that heavier loads (more than about 80% of max, roughly 8 reps or fewer when taken near failure) give the biggest strength gains [1] [11] [26]. Heavy home loads are often not available: a single 12 to 16 kg bell is light for a squat. So the app should move users to harder variations rather than inflate reps when the goal is strength. ACSM 2009 recommends 8 to 12 reps for novices while they learn [2].

**Reps for balanced and muscle growth.** When sets are taken to or near failure, muscle growth is similar from about 30% to 100% of max load [1] [10] [11]. One trial in trained men found 20 to 25 reps grew muscle as well as 8 to 12 [12]. Very light loads are the exception: 20% of max was clearly worse [13]. With light loads, effort matters more than total volume [14]. Most of these studies are in young men.

**Reps for endurance and conditioning.** ACSM recommends 10 to 25 reps with short rests for local muscular endurance [2], and 15 to 20 reps in its general fitness guidance [3]. The 2026 overview found too little data to say which prescription improves endurance best, only that resistance training improves it [1]. Treat these as consensus.

**Effort (RIR).** Training to failure does not add strength or muscle gains on average [1] [17]. Muscle growth does rise as sets end closer to failure, while strength does not depend much on it [16]. Stopping at 1 to 2 RIR grew muscle as well as failure in trained people [18]. ACSM 2026 suggests about 2 to 3 RIR and notes that RIR scales suit band and bodyweight training [1]. In the older-adult kettlebell trial, the ramp-in was 2 weeks at easy effort before moving to "hard to very hard", with maximal effort discouraged [61].

**Sets per exercise.** Two sets beat one and one beats none. Beyond about 2 to 3 sets per exercise, strength returns diminish [1]. ACSM 2009 suggests 1 to 3 sets for novices and multiple sets for progression [2]; ACSM 2011 suggests 2 to 4 for most adults [3].

**Rest between sets.** Short rests (under 60 s) still build strength. Trained lifters gain more with more than 2 min, while untrained people do not need more than 60 to 120 s [6]. In trained men, 3 min beat 1 min for both strength and growth [8]. For growth, resting more than 60 s gives a small benefit and more than 90 s gives no clear extra [9]. ACSM 2009 recommends at least 2 to 3 min for heavy core lifts and 1 to 2 min for assistance exercises [2], and the NSCA's trainer guidance says the same [5]. However, ACSM 2026's pooled reviews found rest length did not change strength outcomes [1], so these values are sensible rather than critical. The evidence is mostly from men lifting barbells.

**Supersets.** A 2025 meta-analysis (19 studies) found supersets keep the same reps and volume load in less time, with similar long-term strength, endurance and growth. Pairing opposing muscles (push with pull) worked best [31]. The trade-off is higher perceived effort and more fatigue [31].

**Weekly volume.** Muscle growth rises with weekly sets: each extra set added about 0.37% growth in one meta-regression [20]. ACSM 2026 sets 10 or more sets per muscle per week as the growth threshold, with diminishing returns at about 18 to 20 [1]. The largest dose-response analysis (67 studies) found that counting indirect sets as half a set predicted results best, and that strength flattens sooner than growth [22]. For time-poor users, 4 or more sets per muscle per week is a reasonable floor [27].

**Sessions per week.** WHO and ACSM both say at least 2 days a week of muscle-strengthening work covering all major muscle groups [1] [4]. For adults aged 65 and over, WHO recommends functional balance and strength training on 3 or more days a week [4]. With weekly volume equal, frequency does not meaningfully change muscle growth [24]. Strength gains rise with frequency, but mostly because of the extra volume [25]; the newest analysis finds strength gains rise with frequency, with diminishing returns, but no clear effect on growth [22]. ACSM 2011 suggests 48 h before training the same muscle group again [3].

**Rep speed.** ACSM 2009 recommends moderate speed (1 to 2 s up, 1 to 2 s down) for most training [2]. ACSM 2026 found that rep duration from 0.5 s to 8 s did not change muscle growth [1]. The timed mode can therefore safely use about 3 s per rep to turn rep targets into countdowns.

**Circuit work:rest.** No study compares work:rest ratios for home circuits directly. What exists is a set of formats that each worked in small trials: 30/10 bodyweight circuits [45] [46], 20/10 whole-body intervals [49] [50], 30/30 kettlebell swings [55] and 15/15 kettlebell snatches [56]. The app's 30 to 45 s work with 15 to 20 s rest sits inside this range. Beginners do better with more rest, and the 2023 trial in sedentary women also started with only one circuit [45].

**Transition time.** The bodyweight circuit trials used a 10 s break between stations [45]. ACSM's guidance for circuit weight training is simply "the time needed to get from one station to the next" [2]. A 5 s gap is too short to read the next move and get into position; 10 s is the best-supported default, and 15 to 20 s when equipment changes (practitioner).

**Rounds and rest between rounds.** Trials increased rounds gradually: 1 to 5 circuits over 8 weeks [45], and 1 to 2 circuits [46]. Rounds were done back to back, so a 60 s break between rounds is a comfort default, not a tested value. Circuit resistance training produced large aerobic gains when sessions lasted at least about 20 to 30 min over 6 to 12 weeks [63]. Short circuits still help, but expect smaller effects.

**Warm-up.** Warm-ups improved performance in 79% of measures across 32 studies [33]. High-load, movement-specific warm-ups have the strongest support for upper-body strength and power [34]. A general or specific warm-up did not change reps done at 80% of max [35], and the reviews found no good evidence that warm-ups prevent injuries [3] [34]. Static stretches of 60 s or more per muscle cut performance by about 4.6%, while shorter ones cost about 1% [36]. Hence the advice: dynamic moves plus a lighter ramp set. The 3 to 5 min length is practitioner guidance; trials used 2 min [53] and 5 min [45] warm-ups.

**Cool-down and stretching.** Stretching before or after exercise does not meaningfully reduce soreness [37]. Post-exercise stretching did not improve strength recovery or soreness [38], and active cool-downs do not help most recovery markers [39]. Stretching does improve range of motion over time. ACSM recommends holds of 10 to 30 s (30 to 60 s for older adults), about 60 s total per stretch, on at least 2 to 3 days a week, done after training or on its own [3]. Resistance training itself also improves range of motion [27].

**Which poses (practitioner choice).** Reps fills the optional cool-down with yoga holds rather than standing or doorway stretches: pigeon, happy baby and bridge, plus prop-supported poses from the Katonah Yoga home practice (hero seat with cow-face arms, reclined hero, supported fish over a block, legs resting on a chair) [64]. Katonah Yoga is a Hatha-based practice that holds poses and uses props rather than flowing between them. No trial compares these poses with other stretches; the choice is a preference. The dose still follows ACSM: 30 s holds with 10 s to change position [3]. Holds run kneeling, then lying, then a final rest, so you only get down to the floor once.

**Progression.** ACSM 2009 recommends a 2 to 10% load increase once the user beats the rep target by 1 to 2 reps in two consecutive sessions [2]. In trained lifters, adding reps at the same load worked as well as adding load over 8 weeks [43]. That suits fixed kettlebell and dumbbell weights. ACSM 2026 adds that progression is not needed to get benefits, but is needed for continued long-term progress [1].

**Deloads.** The only consensus comes from a panel of expert strength and physique coaches. They defined a deload as "a period of reduced training stress", done by cutting sets, reps or frequency, and placed flexibly in the training cycle [40]. Competitive lifters report deloading for about 6 days every 5.6 weeks (plus or minus 2.3), cutting sets and reps, adding RIR and keeping exercises the same [41]. In the only trial, a full week off in the middle of a 9-week programme did not change muscle growth and slightly reduced strength gains [42]. A planned light week every 4 to 6 weeks is a reasonable default, but it is not proven.

**Exercise rotation.** ACSM 2026 could not determine whether varying exercises changes results [1]. Coaches agree that exercises can either stay the same or change at a new block [40]. Keep each block's exercises fixed so the double-progression rule has something to track.

**Short sessions.** Minimal doses work. Single-set training and once-a-week training are the best-supported low-time approaches, and short "exercise snacks" look promising [29]. A single set of 6 to 12 reps, 2 to 3 times a week, raised 1RM in trained men, though less than more volume [30]. For time-limited lifting, prioritise multi-joint moves, use supersets, keep the warm-up specific, and skip stretching unless flexibility is a goal [27] [31].

**Light equipment only.** Light loads build muscle as well as heavy ones only if sets go near failure [10] [14]. Maximal strength still improves less [10] [11] [14]. Bands gave strength gains similar to weights in a small meta-analysis (8 studies, some in clinical groups) [32]. Bands are less proven for heavy lower-body work [27]. Slower lowering and pauses make a light weight feel heavier so users reach near-failure in fewer reps (practitioner), but rep speed itself does not add growth [1].
